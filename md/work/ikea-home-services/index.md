Context

## Extending IKEA beyond furniture

IKEA's New Business Platform is an internal startup unit within Ingka Group, tasked with expanding the brand into adjacent markets across Europe. The ambition was clear: if you've just bought a flat-pack wardrobe, you probably need someone to help you put it up. And IKEA wanted to be the one to connect you.

IKEA Home Services is the result: a peer-to-peer marketplace connecting homeowners with vetted, reviewed professionals across more than 50 home improvement and assembly categories. It launched in Madrid in September 2024, reached Barcelona a year later, and expanded to seven Spanish regions by May 2026, including renovation services added in March 2026.

_Both sides of the marketplace must be convinced simultaneously: IKEA's platform acts as the trust layer that makes quality, pricing, and safety legible to both homeowners and professionals._

_**Two sides, one platform.** AI-assisted matching turns a homeowner's natural-language request into a structured job (left), while a verified onboarding flow brings professionals onto the supply side (right)._

The challenge

## Trust, consistency, and speed

Marketplace products live or die on trust. Homeowners need to trust the professionals they're letting into their homes. Professionals need to trust that the platform is worth their time. And IKEA needs the product to feel like IKEA: considered, reliable, without friction.

The additional complexity: we were building a new product inside a very large, brand-sensitive organisation, working with multiple stakeholders across product, service design, engineering, content, and the in-store IKEA network. Decisions had to be made at pace, but they had to hold up to scrutiny. The brief was never fully stable.

Trust, made explicit

Rather than leaving credibility implicit, IKEA's commitment is stated plainly at the point of decision: verified professionals, backed by the brand, with recourse if something goes wrong.

Approach

## Research first, iteration always

We ran user research sessions inside IKEA stores: recruiting participants on-site and testing flows in context, with the app running on real devices. This gave us faster signal than remote testing and was consistent with the product's natural discovery channel.

The design process was iterative by necessity. A marketplace has two distinct user types, homeowners and service professionals, each with different mental models and success criteria. We mapped flows for both sides separately before working on the points of intersection: booking, confirmation, dispute resolution, and review mechanics.

Accessibility was treated as a requirement from day one, not an audit at the end. The product had to meet IKEA's own accessibility standards alongside WCAG AA, across iOS and Android.

_**Discovery.** Search by need, browse the services within a category, and review a verified professional's profile. Each step builds confidence before the homeowner commits to anything._

_**The booking moment.** IKEA Family sign-in as step one ties the request to the wider ecosystem, a considered loading state keeps the user oriented, and a confirmation that sets expectations clearly closes the loop._

Decisions

## Three decisions that shaped the work

### Run the research inside IKEA stores

Why

Recruiting on site and testing flows on real devices gave faster signal than remote testing, and matched how people find the product anyway.

### Map the two sides separately, then design where they meet

Why

Homeowners and professionals have different mental models and different ideas of success. Each side's flows came first; booking, confirmation, dispute resolution and review mechanics came after.

### Treat accessibility as a requirement from the first flow

Why

The product had to meet IKEA's own accessibility standards and WCAG AA across iOS and Android, so it was a requirement from day one rather than an audit at the end.

Learnings

## What building inside a large brand teaches you

Being embedded inside IKEA's team has confirmed something for me: the design itself is rarely the hard part. Keeping a coherent direction while stakeholder priorities shift, the roadmap moves, and people rotate on and off the team. That's the actual job. Fighting that instability is a losing battle. Building systems that can absorb it isn't.

This project also sharpened how I think about trust in a marketplace. Visual polish and a smooth onboarding flow barely register next to things like how a professional's credentials get displayed, what happens on screen when a booking goes wrong, or the exact wording of a review prompt. Nobody labels those as "design decisions," but that's exactly what they are, and they carry most of the weight.
