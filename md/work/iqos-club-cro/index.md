## A loyalty programme with a specific kind of complexity

IQOS Club is Philip Morris International's loyalty programme for IQOS users in Spain, a members' portal where users track rewards, access exclusive content, manage their devices, and engage with the brand between purchases. The programme sits in the tobacco alternatives category, which carries significant marketing restrictions and shapes what's possible in digital promotion.

Acquisition wasn't really the CRO challenge here. Engagement and retention were: getting existing members to use the platform more, move up through loyalty tiers, and stay connected to the brand over the long haul. That calls for a different set of levers than a standard transactional funnel.

The challenge

## Low traffic, high standards

Loyalty portals are structurally harder to optimise than transactional funnels. Traffic volumes are lower, because you're targeting only existing members, not the broader market. This means experiments take longer to reach statistical significance, and the risk of acting on false positives is higher.

In this environment, good CRO practice requires more discipline, not less. The hypothesis quality has to be higher because you can run fewer experiments. The design decisions have to be better informed because you have less data to course-correct with.

Approach

## Behavioural economics in a loyalty context

The most relevant behavioural frameworks for loyalty are different from those that dominate conversion optimisation in e-commerce. Loss aversion still applies: the framing of tier status and expiring points is a powerful lever. But habit formation mechanics become more important here: variable reward schedules, completion mechanics, progress visualisation.

As project lead, I was responsible for generating the experiment pipeline: identifying opportunity areas through analytics and heuristic review, structuring hypotheses, prioritising based on potential impact and implementation cost, and communicating the programme's direction to the client. The design work included experiment variant design, interaction specifications, and performance analysis.

The experiment pipeline ran in three phases: insight generation (web analytics, heatmaps, heuristic review, and behavioural economics framing), hypothesis and prioritisation (structuring focused hypotheses ranked by impact versus implementation cost), and rapid experimentation using Visual Website Optimizer (VWO). VWO enabled both A/B tests and personalisation variants without heavy development overhead, a key constraint given the shared-resource model. Personalisation complemented A/B work: tailoring content and mechanics to member segments, rather than forcing a single optimised experience on a heterogeneous membership base.

"In loyalty work, 'will people convert' is the easy question. The one that actually matters is whether you're building a habit or just catching them in a good mood."

Outcome

## Ongoing programme with measurable engagement gains

The IQOS Club engagement improved across the programme period. Specific figures are subject to client confidentiality and have not been published. The programme ran continuously throughout the Flat 101 engagement.

The more durable outcome for me was methodological: running CRO at low traffic volumes sharpens your thinking about what makes a good hypothesis and what constitutes meaningful evidence. The discipline of making high-quality decisions with limited data is transferable to almost any design context.

Decisions

## Three decisions that shaped the work

### Raise the quality of each hypothesis instead of running more tests

Why

Only existing members visit the portal, so experiments take longer to reach significance and a false positive is more likely to be acted on. With fewer experiments possible, each one has to be better argued.

Cost

Fewer experiments could run.

Result

A pipeline that ranked hypotheses by impact against implementation cost, built on analytics, heatmaps and heuristic review.

### Run variants in VWO so nothing waited for a development sprint

Why

Development resources were shared with other work, and VWO could run both A/B tests and personalisation without heavy engineering.

Result

The client came to use VWO as a fast channel for launch campaigns, with modals live within hours of a product launch.

### Personalise by segment instead of optimising one experience

Why

The membership base is heterogeneous, and the alternative was forcing one optimised experience on all of it.

Result

Members with 2,500+ points see a redeem call to action; those under 500 see earn-oriented ones. Same hero, different primary intent.

Scope evolution

## CRO tooling as a campaign delivery channel

As the programme matured, something useful happened: the client began recognising VWO not just as a testing tool, but as a fast deployment channel for time-sensitive campaign assets. Because VWO variants could go live without a development sprint, it became the preferred path for promotional modals, campaign banners, and temporary content tied to specific launches.

This was a natural evolution. The same infrastructure that let us test UX hypotheses could push a promotional overlay to logged-in members within hours of a product launch, without touching the underlying platform. Promotional modals for device releases and loyalty campaigns became a recurring deliverable alongside the A/B pipeline.
